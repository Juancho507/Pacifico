Explore the Pacific

The Pacific Region

A world of ocean, rainforest, rivers, mangroves, and vibrant Afro-Colombian and Indigenous heritage.

The Colombian Pacific at a Glance

The Pacific is one of Colombia's six natural regions: a long, narrow strip between the Western Andes and the ocean, from Panama to Ecuador. It is the country's wettest region and one of the rainiest places on Earth.

4departments: Chocó, Valle del Cauca, Cauca, Nariño
83,000+km² of surface area
1,000+km of Pacific coastline
~13,000mm of rain per year in Lloró, Chocó
One country, shared culture. The Colombian Pacific lies entirely within Colombia, but its cultural roots cross borders: Panama's Darién to the north and Ecuador's Esmeraldas to the south share music, dances, oral traditions, and even the myth of La Tunda. In 2015, UNESCO inscribed the marimba music, songs, and dances of southern Colombia and Esmeraldas together.

Geography, Rivers & Climate

Landscapes

  • Coastal plain, Darién and Baudó mountain ranges, and the Atrato River valley.
  • Main rivers: Atrato, San Juan, Patía, Baudó, and Iscuandé. They are the region's highways — people travel by boat, canoe, or panga.
  • Quiet bays, beaches, cliffs, coral reefs, and one of South America's largest mangrove belts.

Why is it so rainy?

Warm, moisture-filled winds from the Pacific hit the Western Andes, rise, cool, and condense into clouds. The result is heavy rain almost all year. Lloró (Chocó) averages about 13,000 mm per year and competes with López de Micay (Cauca) for the title of the wettest place on the planet.

People & History

The Pacific is home mainly to Afro-Colombian and Indigenous communities. Long before the Spanish arrived in the 16th century, Emberá and Awá peoples lived here. During colonial times, enslaved Africans were forced to work in gold mining; from this encounter, the culture of the Litoral Pacífico was born.

Before the 16th century

Indigenous peoples such as the Emberá, Awá, Wounaan, and Tule live across the region.

16th–19th centuries

Enslaved Africans are brought to work in gold extraction.

1851

Slavery is abolished in Colombia.

1991

The new Constitution recognizes Colombia as multiethnic and pluricultural; transitional Article 55 opens the door for Black community rights.

1993

Law 70 recognizes Black communities as an ethnic group and their right to collective land in rural Pacific river areas; Community Councils are created.

2010

"Marimba music and traditional songs of the southern Pacific" enter Colombia's Representative List of Intangible Cultural Heritage.

2015

UNESCO inscribes the marimba music, songs, and dances of southern Colombia and Esmeraldas (Ecuador) as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.

Indigenous peoples

Emberá (Dóbida, Chamí, Katío, Eperara Siapidara), Wounaan, Awá, and Tule (Guna) keep languages, basketry, beadwork, and jagua body painting alive.

Afro-Colombian heritage

After abolition, Black communities settled along rivers and the coast, developing artisanal fishing, traditional mining, rooftop gardens, plantain fields, healing traditions, oral poetry, music, and patron-saint festivals.

Population figures depend on how the region is counted: about 1.5 million people in the coastal and rainforest strip; more than 8 million if the four complete departments (including Cali) are counted.

Myths & Legends

Oral stories teach respect for the forest, water, and elders. They are not just entertainment: they carry community rules and memory.

🌪️

La Tunda

Chocó, Pacific coast, and Esmeraldas (Ecuador)

A shape-shifting being who disguises herself as a woman or a relative to lure people into the forest. Legends say one of her legs always looks like a wooden chocolate whisk (molinillo). A person taken by her is called entundado.

💧

La Madre de Agua

Rivers and streams

A spirit who sings beside the water. According to tradition, a child who hears her song becomes hypnotized and enters the water looking for her. That is why children are warned not to play alone near rivers.

⛵

Riviel & the Buque Maravelí

Pacific oral tradition

Other well-known stories include the Riviel and the Buque Maravelí, a ghost ship. They are passed down orally from generation to generation.

Crafts & Identity

Basketry

Emberá, Eperara Siapidara, and Wounaan communities weave baskets and canastos from local plant fibers.

Hats

Made in Chocó, Valle, Cauca, and Nariño; for many communities they protect the body and also the spirit.

Goldwork

A legacy of centuries of gold craftsmanship, kept alive today as an identity craft.

Wood carving

Canoes, houses, plates, spoons, and vessels carved by Indigenous and Afro-Colombian communities.

Marimba de chonta

Built from chonta palm wood and guadua tubes, it is the signature instrument of the region.

African-inspired fashion

Turbans and garments made with African and Pacific fabric scraps, especially visible at the Petronio Álvarez Festival.

The turban is more than an accessory. In the Pacific it is a sign of identity and resistance. Chocoan designers have brought it to the Petronio Álvarez stage to celebrate Afro-Colombian culture and explain why communities wear it.

Four Festivals to Understand the Pacific

August · Cali

Petronio Álvarez

Named after the composer of "Mi Buenaventura". It includes competitions for marimba, chirimía, Caucan violins, and free groups. The traditional food pavilion brings together about 60 stalls, and the flags of Pacific communities parade (21 in 2023).

Sept–Oct · Quibdó

San Pacho

A patron-saint festival mixing devotion, humor, and disguise. Festival food includes longaniza, carne serrana, coastal cheese, and river fish.

January · Pasto

Carnaval de Negros y Blancos

A festival of inclusion born in the southern Andes: one day to paint yourself black and another to cover yourself in white. The Petronio Álvarez festival has even participated in Black Day.

Feb–March · Tumaco

Carnaval del Fuego

A Nariño coast celebration with parades and music, honoring fire as a symbol of life.

Music: The Sound of the Litoral

Pacific music is mostly percussion and voices: fast rhythms led by drums, a lead singer (cantador or cantadora), and a responding chorus. Its roots are African, with Indigenous and Spanish influences. In 2015, UNESCO recognized the marimba music, songs, and dances of the southern Pacific together with Ecuador.

Marimba de chonta

A xylophone made from chonta palm wood with guadua resonator tubes. It is the queen of the ensemble and has relatives in Africa (the balafon) and Veracruz, Mexico.

Bombo & tambora

Large, deep drums that mark the pulse of the marimba ensemble.

Cununos (male & female)

Cone-shaped drums, one higher and one lower, played with the hands.

Guasá

A guadua or metal tube filled with seeds, shaken like a maraca; it adds a soft, rain-like sound.

Chirimía

Clarinet, snare drum, and cymbals join the bombo to form the chirimía chocoana, a typical street band.

Modern Pacific sound

ChocQuibTown (Chocó) and groups such as Herencia de Timbiquí and Renacer de Timbiquí bring marimba sounds to global stages, mixing them with other genres.

Currulao — king of the southern Pacific Abozao — joyful celebration Juga, bunde & arrullos — devotion and ritual Alabaos — a cappella funeral songs Chirimía chocoana — street music Patacoré, aguabajo & berejú — traditional rhythms

Traditional Dances

Currulao

A couple or group courtship dance: the man follows the woman through turns and gestures, accompanied by marimba, cununos, bombo, and guasá.

Abozao

A joyful celebration dance with shouts and exclamations, lighter than currulao.

Jota chocoana

A Chocoan dance with heritage from the Spanish jota.

Juga & aguabajo

Dances linked to festivals and songs, with choruses and drums.

Caderona & mapalé

High-energy hip dances kept alive by young people in Tumaco alongside currulao.

Comparsas & parades

At San Pacho, Petronio, and carnivals, whole groups parade with costumes, masks, and music.

Try it: currulao is learned by watching. Notice how the couple moves closer and farther apart with the bombo, how the woman plays with her skirt, and how the man answers with short steps. Cali offers workshops and dance sessions to learn it.

Traditional Clothing

Afro-Colombian women

A two-piece festive outfit: a blouse (sometimes embroidered or ruffled) and a wide skirt just below the knee. Bright colors, necklaces, earrings, and a turban or headscarf.

Afro-Colombian men

White shirt and white trousers with a hat. White dominates for its simplicity and freshness in the humid heat.

Emberá & Wounaan

Women wear the paruma (a colorful cloth skirt) and bead necklaces. Jagua body painting is common.

Andean Nariño

In Andean Nariño, the apanga is worn with thicker fabrics and warm layers because of the cold. The name comes from Quechua llapangua ("barefoot").

Turbans

In the Pacific, the turban is a sign of identity and resistance. Many garments are made with scraps of African and Pacific fabrics.

Jagua painting

For Emberá communities, jagua has ritual value: it is used for festivals, healing ceremonies, and rites of passage. Designs and body placement vary by gender and marital status, and the dye lasts about eight days.

A Day of Food in Guapi

  • Breakfast: black coffee and tapao de pescado (fresh, smoked, or salted) with boiled plantain or banana.
  • Lunch: fish sancocho or sudado with coconut milk, coconut rice, and fried or boiled plantain; also crab, shrimp, or clam sudado.
  • Dinner or snack: fried fish or smoked bushmeat tapao with fried plantain and aguapanela.

Fruits, sweets & other drinks

  • Chontaduro is a palm fruit eaten cooked; borojó, naidí, milpesos, lulo, and guayaba are used in juices and desserts.
  • Other drinks include hot aguapanela, Pacific fruit juices, and coffee.
  • For year-end celebrations in Nariño: fried coconut fish, fish sancocho, coconut-breaded shrimp, carne serrana tapao, and corvina in chontaduro sauce.
Recipe: Encocado de Pescado (4 people)

Ingredients: 800 g fish fillets (snapper, corvina, or róbalo); 400 ml coconut milk; 1 bunch spring onion; 2 garlic cloves; 1 teaspoon achiote; fresh cilantro and poleo or other herbs; salt, pepper, and lemon.

  1. Marinate the fish with lemon, salt, and pepper for 15 minutes.
  2. Sauté onion, garlic, and achiote until fragrant.
  3. Add coconut milk and herbs; cook on low heat for 5 minutes.
  4. Add the fish, cover, and cook for 8–10 minutes without stirring too much.
  5. Serve with coconut rice and patacones.

Home-style, simplified version; every Pacific family has its own seasoning and texture.

Note: Viche, curao, tomaseca, and arrechón are traditional drinks made with alcohol; for a school project we mention them only as part of the culture and do not offer them.

More Unique Species

🦈

Hammerhead Sharks

Malpelo Sanctuary · Sanquianga

Malpelo, a UNESCO Natural World Heritage Site since 2006, gathers hundreds of hammerhead sharks and is a world-class diving destination. Critically endangered hammerheads have also been recorded near Sanquianga.

🐆

Jaguar

Valle del Cauca Pacific corridor

The entire Pacific side of Valle del Cauca is part of the jaguar corridor for Panthera onca, the largest feline in the Americas.

🐋

Whale Sharks & Manta Rays

Gorgona · Malpelo

Devil rays, rays, whale sharks, whitetip sharks, and blacktip sharks frequent the Pacific islands.

🦪

Piangua & Mangrove Life

Mangroves

Piangua, the black mangrove clam, is harvested by piangüeras; shrimp, jaibas, and crabs support the cuisine of the Litoral.

🐢

Hawksbill Turtle

Open sea and beaches

The hawksbill turtle, with its serrated shell edges, travels these waters. Several sea turtle species are endangered due to habitat loss.

🐦

Endemic Biodiversity

Chocó biogeográfico

Utría receives hundreds of migratory birds each year. Valle del Cauca alone has about 350 amphibian and reptile species; around 2,000 Pacific plant and animal species are estimated to be endemic.

Nature Fun Facts

When is whale season?

From July to October, humpback whales arrive from Antarctica to Colombia's Pacific.

Why is Chocó special?

It's among the world's most biodiverse regions, with extreme rainfall and unique species.

What makes mangroves important?

They protect shores from erosion and provide habitat for many marine and bird species.

Can you see turtles in this region?

Yes — sea turtles can be observed around Nuquí and Isla Gorgona.

How to Get There

By air

Several Chocó destinations (Nuquí, Bahía Solano) are reached by short flights, including charter flights from Medellín.

By road

Buenaventura is reached by road from Cali; Tumaco is reached from Pasto.

By boat

Rivers are the region's highways. Expect boat, canoe, or panga transfers where there are no roads.

Climate & Packing

Expect the unexpected

The climate is unpredictable and humid. Pack a raincoat, light clothing, sunscreen, and shoes that can get wet.

Travel light, travel ready

Bring a reusable water bottle, insect repellent, a dry bag for boat rides, and cash for local purchases where cards are not accepted.

Respect & Safety

Respect communities

Ask permission before taking photos, buy crafts directly from the people who make them, and support community tourism.

Travel informed

Some areas have been affected by armed conflict. Check official recommendations and local operators before traveling.

Protect the environment

Do not leave trash in mangroves or on beaches, and keep a respectful distance from whales and other wildlife.

Book responsibly

During whale season, choose authorized local operators and follow the Navy's safety and observation rules.

Wow Facts

🌧️

The rainiest place

Lloró, Chocó, receives about 13,000 mm of rain per year and is among the wettest places on the planet. Its demonym is lloroseño.

🐋

An 8,500 km journey

Humpback whales swim about 8,500 km from Antarctica to Colombian waters to give birth.

🌿

A biodiversity treasure

WWF identifies the Chocó biogeográfico as the place with the highest biodiversity per square kilometer in the world.

🪘

A marimba with sisters

The African balafon and the marimba of Veracruz, Mexico, are "relatives" of the Pacific marimba de chonta.

🐸

The most venomous vertebrate

The golden poison frog (Phyllobates terribilis) is considered the most venomous vertebrate on the planet.

🏛️

World heritage

Malpelo (2006) and the marimba music of southern Colombia and Esmeraldas (2015) have UNESCO recognition.

🍲

A dish born from slavery

Tapao was created centuries ago by Afro-descendant communities in Cauca, Valle, and Chocó during slavery.

🥄

A molinillo leg

La Tunda's telltale feature: one leg always looks like a molinillo, the wooden whisk used to beat chocolate.

🎶

UNESCO 2015

The marimba music, songs, and dances of southern Colombia and Esmeraldas were inscribed together as Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity.
